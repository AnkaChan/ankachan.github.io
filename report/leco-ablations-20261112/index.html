LeCO ablation results

Every model is trained for 500,000 steps with the same data and then runs the same test scene. This page shows which changes still give a usable solver at inference, and which do not.

Findings

  1. A per-vertex step scale does not work at inference. Every model with a per-node scale breaks within the first 60 to 180 of 4,040 steps. This holds at 5, 10 and 30 iterations, with DAT and without DAT, with per-node gradient normalization, with the gradient magnitude input, and with a ring context of 1 to 32 rings. The truncated scale does not rescue them: the positions stay finite, but more than 5 % of the edges are badly stretched after 60 to 180 steps.
  2. Per-node gradient normalization with a global scale works. It finishes every setting. At 5 iterations it is at least as good as the baseline (end distance 21 to 26 mm against 32 to 49 mm, depth 1.6 to 2.9 mm against 1.8 to 10.3 mm). At 10 and 30 iterations the two are equal. Two runs per model are not enough to call it better, and on the criteria of the paper (gradient norm and residual ratio per step) it is level with the baseline.
  3. The gradient magnitude input does not help. With global normalization it makes the 5-iteration result clearly worse (109 to 116 mm from the reference at 25 % of the sequence). At 10 iterations it equals the baseline. With per-node normalization the result is about the same as the baseline.
  4. Width 96 or more is safe. Below 96 the 5-iteration solver is not reliable. Widths 96, 128, 192 and 256 give the same quality. Width 64 keeps good edge numbers but loses a garment at 5 iterations. Width 32 breaks, width 24 gives NaN, width 16 ends far off. At 10 iterations every width finishes. No width above 96 is better than 96.
  5. The other ablations confirm the main design. Each removed input makes the result worse or unstable. The per-iteration scale and the model without scale finish, but with lower accuracy. A context window of 3 equals the baseline within the run-to-run spread.
  6. A context window of 5 gives the closest 5-iteration result. Its end distance is 20 to 21 mm, against 32 to 49 mm for the baseline and 21 to 26 mm for per-node normalization. It costs 5 message-passing rounds per iteration, so it is not a free gain.
  7. The fixed-state test of the code base separates two things. Almost every model can solve a single time step in 30 iterations, also the per-node scale models. Their failure appears only in rollouts. From a cold start the per-node normalization with the magnitude input is the fastest model (6 % of the energy decrease missing after 5 iterations, baseline 31 %).
  8. On the criteria of the paper no ablation beats the baseline at 5 iterations. Objective, gradient norm and residual ratio per time step are level for all stable models. At 10 iterations the larger context windows are about 30 % lower; at 30 iterations all are equal.

Still open: the model without iteration (in training).

How to read the tables

Test

Scene dlayers_00396 (layered dress on a moving body), 4,040 solver steps. DAT is on with dynamic collision detection. “5 it · 15 mm” means 5 network iterations per step and a 15 mm collision query radius. Every model runs on the same PyTorch network path. Each line in a cell is one run.

Numbers

edge
Largest edge stretch in the run (length / rest length). The baseline reaches about 2 to 6.
depth
Deepest body penetration in the run, in mm.
dist.
Median vertex distance to the reference, in mm, at 25 % of the sequence / at the end. The reference is the released checkpoint at 30 iterations.

Outcome

OK
Finished, close to the reference (below 50 mm at 25 % and below 150 mm at the end).
far off
Finished, but far from the reference. lost means more than 1 m: a garment left the body.
NaN
Positions became NaN at this step.
broken
Stopped at this step: more than 5 % of the edges were badly stretched.
time limit
No result: the run was too slow on a GPU shared with trainings. It is shown only if no rerun gave a result.

Criteria of the paper

The three criteria of the paper, recorded at every time step of the rollout on dlayers_00396 (4,040 steps, DAT on, 15 mm): the objective G after K iterations, the gradient norm ‖∇XG‖2 over the free vertices, and the gradient norm after K iterations divided by the gradient norm at the start of the step. The recorder is the one of the code base (experiments/ablation_statistics/run.py), changed only to accept a sewn scene. Both gradients use the final contact set of the step. Each metric has its mean and its median over the steps. A run counts as failed when any value becomes non-finite, as in the summary script of the code base. Green is 30 % or more below the baseline, red is 50 % or more above; G has no colour.

Reading

  • At 5 iterations no stable model is clearly below the baseline in gradient norm or in the residual ratio. Per-node normalization is level with it (median ratio 0.127 against 0.123), and so are widths 128 to 256 and both context windows. The magnitude input has a lower ratio but a 4 times larger gradient norm: its run drifted away, so its start gradients are larger and its ratio is not comparable.
  • At 10 iterations the context windows 3 and 5 are about 30 % lower in median gradient norm and ratio. The other stable models stay level with the baseline.
  • At 30 iterations all stable models with the full inputs are equal: median gradient norm about 0.01 to 0.02, median ratio 0.001 to 0.004.
  • Failed by the strict rule: every per-node scale model at every K, the last update divided by its RMS, the model without the current gradient at 10 and 30 iterations, and widths 16, 24 and 32 at 5 iterations.
  • The mean gradient norm is several times the median in every row. A few steps with large gradients dominate the mean, so the median is the steadier number.
  • G is comparable only while the trajectories stay together. A garment that slides down lowers G: width 64 and the magnitude input at 5 iterations have the lowest G and are the runs that ended far from the reference.

Fixed-state test

The direct test of the code base (experiments/checkpoints/checkpoint_progress.py). Every model solves the same 300 saved states, each one time step, with fresh optimizer history. The states come from two motions of the repository's test split, simulated by the baseline at 60 iterations. Numbers are in percent; lower is better. “5 it”, “10 it”, “30 it”: share of the energy decrease that is still missing, median over the states. 100 % of the decrease is what the baseline reaches after 60 iterations on the same state. A value above 100 means the energy went up. “resid.”: gradient norm after 10 iterations relative to the start. “failed”: share of states with a non-finite result. Green is clearly better than the baseline, red is clearly worse. Two limits. The optimizer starts cold in every state (step scale 1e-4, no history), so the 5-iteration column mostly shows how fast a model finds its step size; in a rollout the history carries over. The trainings used all motions (split: all), so this is a like-for-like loss on fixed states, not a held-out test.

Reading

  • All models except three solve a single step: after 30 iterations they reach the same energy as the baseline. The exceptions are the model without gradient inputs (it never lowers the energy), the model without the current gradient and the model without a step scale (each 5 % missing).
  • Per-node scale models do not fail here. They start badly (after 5 iterations the energy is far above the start) and are level with the baseline after 30 iterations. Their rollouts break within 200 steps. So the per-node scale can solve one step from a cold start; the failure is in the rollout, where the scale carries over from step to step. More rings make the cold start better: 1 ring 34 % missing after 10 iterations, 32 rings 5 %.
  • Fastest from a cold start: per-node normalization with the magnitude input (6 % missing after 5 iterations, baseline 31 %). The magnitude input alone and the model without the previous gradient are also faster than the baseline here.
  • Per-node normalization without the magnitude input is slower from a cold start (66 % missing after 5 iterations), although its rollouts are the best. The magnitude input removes this.
  • Width has a small effect: 16 is the slowest; 96 to 256 are equal after 10 iterations.
  • The ranking differs from the rollout tables. A cold start rewards a fast step-size search; a rollout rewards stability over thousands of steps. Both are needed.

Gradient normalization × step scale

Gradient normalization: the gradient input is divided by the RMS of the whole mesh (global) or by the size of the gradient of that vertex (per node). Step scale: one learned scale per axis for the whole mesh (global) or one per vertex (per node). Magnitude input: log10 of the divisor as one more input channel.

  • Widths 16 to 32 need 10 iterations to finish, and 16 and 24 are then less accurate.
  • Widths 128, 192 and 256 give no clear gain over 96 on this scene.

Reading

  • All global-scale runs restart about 36 episodes per 1,000 steps. This is the normal rate.
  • Per-node scale runs restart more often: 100 for the plain per-node scale, 41 to 64 with a ring context.
  • The model without gradient inputs did not learn (loss above 100). The model without iteration restarts most often.
  • The loss alone does not predict the inference result: widths 32 and 64 have a normal loss but fail at 5 iterations.

Limits of these results

  • One test scene. One or two runs per cell. Two runs of the same model differ visibly (see the baseline rows), so small differences between models mean nothing.
  • No timing on this page. The inference runs shared the GPUs with the trainings, so their step times are not clean. Models of other widths also cannot use the fused width-96 network path yet.
  • No model was trained with DAT. DAT, the 7 mm radius and the truncated scale act at inference only.
  • The baseline row is width_96, retrained with the same configuration as all ablations (neighbor_projection: true, node_update_input: combined).
  • Related report: the same network on a tetrahedral cuboid, without retraining.
